Standards the build enforces, not standards a wiki describes

1,165

automated tests across four suites — backend unit, HTTP end-to-end, frontend, and the mobile client. The e2e suite gates on a real compile and bundle of both apps, because a unit test cannot catch a missing import in a module it mocks.

6 + 8

steps in the production smoke test: six that drive a real message end to end through the live system on every deploy and daily, and eight that clean up after it — the Google event, the local rows, and the test message itself.

0

framework imports permitted outside the adapter and graph layers. The architecture test fails the build otherwise — the standard is executable.

Tracing you can hand to someone else

LangSmith tracing is opt-in and documented as a data-egress decision, not a debug flag: the docs say what leaves the machine when it is on.

Diagnosis as a first-class feature

"Why was my group not captured?" is answered by a log line naming the group, not by attaching a debugger. Counters cover events created, classifier rejections, and each dedup layer's firing rate.

Known gaps are written down with the reason — metrics are point-in-time key-values, not a time series.

Three numbers and a panel. 1,165 tests is the least interesting of them. What I would actually point at is that the e2e suite compiles and bundles both applications, because mocked unit tests cannot catch a missing import in a module they mock. Six-plus-eight is the production smoke test, and the eight is the part people miss: it cleans up after itself. A self-test that leaves artefacts behind becomes the thing you stop running. It deletes the Google event, the local rows and the WhatsApp message it sent. Zero is the architecture test. An executable standard beats a documented one — that is the entire argument for internal tooling, and it is a line in the job description. The right panel, briefly: tracing is framed as data egress rather than a debug switch, which is the framing a bank needs. And the known gaps are written down with reasons — I would rather hand you a document that says "metrics are point-in-time, here is why that is a problem" than one that implies they are fine.
